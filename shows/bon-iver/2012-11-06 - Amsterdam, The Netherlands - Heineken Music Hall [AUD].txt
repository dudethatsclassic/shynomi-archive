NOTES:

Earlier in the day, I had made a half-hearted attempt at flogging my ticket
for this, but soon discovered a glut of the market. The cause? The dregs of
capitalist society, namely shady ticket agencies (oh, I'm sorry; I meant to
say secondary ticket sellers), dumping speculatively purchased tickets en
masse for face value or less.

This was a sold-out gig, but who's to say whether it would still have sold out
if only fans had purchased tickets? First these so-called agencies (which, in
reality, are little more than some cynical opportunist with a mobile phone and
an awkward web site knocked up in five minutes by his brother-in-law) deprive
real fans of tickets by creating artificial scarcity; then they deprive
genuine sellers of the ability to part with their ticket without incurring a
loss.

All you talentless leeches who prowl the Dutch hunting grounds of Marktplaats
-- yes, that means you, Giggotickets, Tickets4u, TicketTribune, Easy Tickets,
GoGo Tickets, Ticket Studio and all of the other scavenging parasites -- I
wouldn't give you the steam from my piss, much less my hard-earned cash.

I'd rather stay in and wash my hair than see you profit by a single cent. Even
if I were desperate to see the artist in question, the knowledge that my
ticket purchase had caused you to prosper would preoccupy me and spoil the
gig. I'd rather spend my money on Gary Glitter CDs; almost.

'Secondary ticket sellers' you euphemistically call yourselves. Let's not
cloud the issue here. You are touts, pure and simple; but you're more
objectionable than the old-fashioned barrow boy street tout, about whom I feel
almost sentimental and warm-hearted in comparison with you. A street tout
sells his wares for over the odds, yes, but his prices are transparent. The
asking price is what the customer eventually pays, and there it ends. There's
no deception involved.

You, on the other hand, charge way over the odds for tickets to 'sold-out'
concerts that often turn out to be anything but, and then hit the customer
with an additional exorbitant 'processing fee'. Not satisfied with that, you
cap it off with an even more absurd postage charge, whose magnitude suggests
that one's purchase will surely be hand-delivered within a couple of hours by
a naked Hollywood starlet bearing a ticket encased in a bar of gold bullion
and offering sex in return for a signature.

Rather than suffer the loss of selling for under face value, I decide to
attend the gig. After all, there must be a reason I bought the ticket in the
first place, right?

And I actually haven't even lost sight of that reason: I wanted to know what
all the fuss was about.

Ever since Justin Vernon disappeared into a shack in Wisconsin to lick his
wounds after his relationship and band fell apart, emerging on the far side of
winter as Bon Iver with 'For Emma, Forever Ago' under his arm, people have
been hailing the man as the best thing since sliced bread. That debut album
rode high on many a critic's best of 2008 chart and the reviews were enticing.

I bought the record, played it and was bored to tears by it. THIS was what all
the fuss was about? How could so many people be so enamoured with a bunch of
dreary falsetto folk tunes? Blasphemy to some readers, I'm sure, but the
record was a huge disappointment to me.

Perhaps I invested too heavily in the background story. The circumstances of
how the album came to be resonated with me. There's nothing like a bit of
heartache and suffering to get the creative juices flowing. Lovelorn and
bedridden with illness, isolated in the harsh winter of middle America, no way
forward but to play the guitar and write lyrics, romantic justice dictates
that it should have been the record that reasserted the artist's uncrushable
spirit and demonstrated the kind of brilliance possible only when one has hit
rock bottom.

It didn't, which is to say that, for many people, it absolutely did, but not
for me. Unlucky.

By all accounts, the Bon Iver live experience is something truly magical, so
in the spirit of not writing things off without a fair trial (or multiple fair
trials, even), this opportunity seemed as good a time as any to check out
Vernon and his band in a live setting.

Only, when the time came, I was tired and reluctant. The weather was dismal
and I didn't feel like going out, particularly not to the Heineken Music Hall,
one of my least favourite venues in Amsterdam, and then to see a band that I
just don't seem able to appreciate.

I resign myself to attending, however, and perk up. My reluctance diminishes.
I'll make the best of it. And who knows? Maybe I'll be really impressed.

It's still pouring with rain as I bike to the car to drive to the venue, which
is situated in a particularly soulless part of Amsterdam.

Bon Iver have expanded to a nine-piece live. They turn out to be a far more
interesting proposition in the flesh than on record, but still fall short of
truly capturing my imagination.

It's not that the songs aren't played to perfection. They are. I really can't
fault the performance (unless I may produce in evidence the use of autotune
for aesthetic purposes). The band put their heart and soul into every number
and the audience lap it up, right down to the last note.

It's the songs themselves that simply don't grab me, with the exception of the
Hinnom, TX/Wash. segue, which does gently tug at my heartstrings.

Don't let my obvious lack of rapture mislead you, however. I treat the
recording of this concert no differently than I would any other and, on this
occasion, with superb results.

The recording is sublime. As distasteful as some of the peripheral aspects of
the Heineken Music Hall are to me, the sound inside the auditorium is usually
excellent and tonight is no exception. If anything, the occasion is exemplary
of just how good the venue can sound in the right hands.

The separation of instruments in the recording is as good as I have heard from
an audience recording. The band were mixed to perfection and someone somewhere
deserves a medal for his work on the night.
